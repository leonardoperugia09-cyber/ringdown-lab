How it works

After two black holes merge, the remnant rings down like a struck bell. The ring is a sum of damped tones: a loud 220 fundamental and a 221 overtone that fades about three times faster. Change the black hole, the loudness and the fit, and see whether the overtone can be detected in noisy data.

      How it works

      1 · What is black-hole ringdown?

      When two black holes merge, they become one new, bigger black hole. At first it is lopsided, so it shakes itself back into shape. While it shakes, it sends out gravitational waves, ripples in spacetime, the way a bell sends out sound after it is struck. This last part of the signal is called the ringdown.

      The ringdown is made of a few "notes". Each note has a pitch (its frequency, f, in hertz) and a fade time (its damping time, τ: how long it takes to shrink to about a third of its size). For a black hole, both depend on just two things: its mass and how fast it spins. A heavier black hole rings lower and longer. A faster-spinning one rings higher and longer.

      Show the maths

      Each note is a damped wave, A is its starting size and φ where it starts in its cycle:

      h(t) = A · e^(−t/τ) · cos(2πft + φ)

      f and τ come from the Berti, Cardoso & Will (2006) fits, with M the mass (converted to seconds) and χ the spin:

      F = f₁ + f₂(1−χ)^f₃ · Q = q₁ + q₂(1−χ)^q₃ · f = F/(2πM) · τ = Q/(πf)

      2 · What are overtones?

      The loudest and longest note is the fundamental (called 220). The next one is the first overtone (called 221). Unlike a bell's overtone, it is not higher-pitched. It rings at almost the same pitch but fades about three times faster, so it only matters in the first few milliseconds after the peak of the signal.

      In this app the signal is exactly these two notes added together. You choose how loud the overtone starts compared with the fundamental, and its phase: whether it starts in step with the fundamental (0°) or against it (180°).

      If you start the fit later, you throw away the beginning of the data. Both notes keep their pitch and fade time, but by then the overtone has faded much more than the fundamental.

      Show the maths

      The signal from the peak is the sum of the two notes:

      h(t) = Σₙ Aₙ e^(−t/τₙ) cos(2πfₙt + φₙ), n = 220, 221

      The fundamental starts with A = 1 and φ = 0; the sliders set A₂₂₁/A₂₂₀ and φ₂₂₁. Starting at t₀, each note's size is multiplied by e^(−t₀/τ) and its phase moves on by 2πft₀.

      3 · How does noise affect detection?

      A detector never records the clean signal. It records the signal plus noise: random wiggles that are there all the time. Real detectors have many kinds of noise. This app uses the simplest: random "white" noise of the same typical size at every moment.

      The signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) says how loud the signal is compared with that noise. At a low SNR the signal is buried; at a high SNR it stands out clearly.

      The overtone is hard to hear for two reasons. It is short, so it adds only a little to the total loudness. And it sounds almost like the fundamental, so a model with only one note can partly imitate it by changing the fundamental's fade time. That trick gives the wrong fade time, an error called bias. So the overtone can spoil your measurement of the fundamental before you can even tell it is there.

      Show the maths

      The noise is Gaussian with σ = 1 per sample (4096 samples per second for 0.1 s), and the signal is scaled so that SNR = √(Σh²)/σ. Ignoring the overtone makes τ₂₂₀ too short when it starts in step (0°) and too long near 180°.

      4 · What does this experiment tell us?

      The app gives four answers, and each one asks a slightly different question:

      • Significance Z (verdict panel): how sure could we be that the overtone is there? It is the overtone's size divided by how uncertain that size is, so Z = 3 means the overtone is three times bigger than its uncertainty. It is a best-case prediction for your settings, not a measurement.
      • Δχ² (fit table and noisy-data experiment): does a two-note model fit this noisy data better than a one-note model, by more than luck? Two notes always fit a bit better, because extra adjustable numbers can follow the noise too. So the overtone only counts as found when the improvement is above 15.70, a value pure noise beat only 1% of the time in tests with the default settings.
      • Residual SNR: how much of the signal can a one-note model not explain? Below 1, the leftover is smaller than the noise, so no method could tell the two models apart.
      • Bias vs noise: is ignoring the overtone a problem? The bias stays the same however loud the signal is, while the error caused by noise shrinks as the signal gets louder. Above some SNR, the wrong model hurts more than the noise does.

      Everything here is a simulation with simple random noise and a perfect two-note signal. It helps you explore the problem; it says nothing about what a real black hole did.

      Show the maths

      Z = A₂₂₁ / σ(A₂₂₁), with σ from the Fisher matrix of the 8-parameter two-mode model. Δχ² = χ²(one mode) − χ²(two modes), with χ² = Σ(data − model)²/σ². Residual SNR = ‖h − best one-mode fit‖/σ (below 1: Lindblom, Owen & Brown 2008).

      Assumptions and limitations

      • Simulated data only. Nothing here is detector data. Presets labelled GW250114-like or GW150914-like borrow published mass, spin and SNR values; amplitudes and phases are assumptions.
      • White Gaussian noise, one detector. Real noise is coloured, has spectral lines and glitches, and real events are seen by a network.
      • Exact two-mode signal from the peak. No higher overtones, no nonlinear effects, and the peak time is known exactly. In real data the peak time is uncertain.
      • Free-frequency fits. Each mode gets its own f and τ (8 parameters). The GR-linked significance ties both modes to one mass and spin (6 parameters).
      • Fisher significance is a best case. It assumes small, Gaussian errors. Below about Z = 2 the overtone's amplitude is not well constrained and real uncertainties are larger.
      • Thresholds are conventions. "3σ" and Δχ² = 15.70 are choices; the Δχ² threshold applies to the project default configuration and is approximate elsewhere.
      • Fits start near the truth. Least-squares fits start within ±10% of the true f and τ; a blind search could find other minima.
      • Fit bounds. f is limited to 20–2000 Hz and τ to 0.01–100 ms in the app.
